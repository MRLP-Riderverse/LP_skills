# Local Youth & Contemporary-Culture Research

Use this skill when the user wants to understand what young people in a country, city, or community are gathering around, making, caring about, or experimenting with—especially beyond mainstream rankings and celebrity coverage.

## Research posture

1. Frame the task as **human-signal recon**, not a generic trend report.
2. Establish the reference date and requested window before searching. Label every result with its publication date; do not silently widen the window.
3. Prefer independent and local outlets: campus papers, city magazines, scene publications, community newsrooms, artist-run publications, and local radio. Prioritize interviews, profiles, venue/collective reports, first-person testimony, and youth-led projects.
4. Exclude Netflix/streaming rankings, celebrity gossip, generic trendicles, institutional summaries with no lived texture, and pure political misery unless the story contains a concrete youth-led cultural response.
5. Aim for three to five high-texture results. If the time window yields fewer than three strong matches, say so and separate older near-matches from in-window results.

## Verification discipline

- Verify the canonical URL and publication date with the source page when possible; otherwise use an exact-title/date search and state the limitation.
- A search snippet supports only what it literally says. Do not infer quotes, ages, organizers, motivations, or article-body details from a title alone.
- Record title, date, outlet, URL, place/community, and evidence-backed lived signal for each candidate.
- Distinguish **youth-led**, **youth-adjacent emerging scene**, and **general local scene**. Never imply that a handful of stories represent all Canadian youth.
- If page extraction or browser access fails, narrow the prose to snippet-supported facts and disclose that full-page verification was unavailable.

## Human texture and anti-isolation analysis

For each result, identify a concrete signal: who met whom; what they built, practiced, or hosted; what space they used; what language, identity, or genre they crossed; or what first-person quote reveals motive or feeling. Then explain why that could matter to someone feeling isolated—e.g., it offers a repeatable place to return, a low-stakes way to participate, a bridge between identities, or proof that loneliness can become a starting point for collaboration. Keep this interpretation clearly marked as analysis rather than source fact.

## Output

Use a compact numbered list or table. Every item should include:

- **Title**
- **Date — outlet**
- **Verified URL**
- **Place/community**
- **Lived human signal** (with inline citation)
- **Why it may matter to someone feeling isolated** (clearly interpretive)

End with a short synthesis of recurring patterns, not a sweeping claim. Include a Sources block when using grounded-citation practice.
