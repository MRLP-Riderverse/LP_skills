# Community Project Positioning

Use this class-level skill when a user is reasoning about how a culturally specific community project, domain, or brand could become strategically valuable as a category develops.

The goal is to translate intuition into a defensible positioning thesis while preserving cultural humility. A distinctive identity can create real optionality, but domain ownership alone is not proof of market value, authority, legitimacy, or future profit.

## Core Distinction: Asset Position vs. Stewardship Position

Always separate two claims that may be emotionally related but are strategically different:

1. **Asset / business position** — the project controls a scarce, memorable, category-relevant identity (for example, a highly specific domain). This can create first-mover brand equity, negotiating leverage, licensing or partnership options, and replacement cost for later entrants.
2. **Stewardship / cultural position** — the project may be well aligned with the community’s values, needs, history, and trust requirements. This is earned through use, relationships, consent, governance, and contribution; it is not automatically conferred by owning the name.

If the user corrects an interpretation, restate the narrower claim accurately rather than defending the earlier framing.

## Positioning Analysis

When evaluating a domain or brand, identify:

- **Specificity:** Does the name naturally identify a place, people, culture, or emerging category?
- **Memorability:** Is it easy to say, spell, recognize, and reuse?
- **Semantic fit:** Does the domain extension or naming structure reinforce the project?
- **Scarcity:** Is the exact identity difficult to replicate or substitute?
- **Incumbency:** Has the project already accumulated association, content, relationships, or public proof around it?
- **Optionality:** Could the owner build, license, partner, redirect, or hold the asset while the category develops?
- **Replacement cost:** How painful would it be for a later entrant to adopt a different identity after the obvious one is established?

Phrase the resulting thesis as conditional and testable:

> If [category or need] becomes meaningful in [place/community], [project] already occupies a natural identity position because [specific reasons].

## Domain-Asset Guardrails

Do not claim that a domain is already an “incredibly expensive asset” without valuation evidence or comparable sales. Instead distinguish:

- **Strategic value:** leverage, recognizability, optionality, and category ownership;
- **Realized commercial value:** revenue, offers, licensing interest, acquisition bids, or documented comparable transactions.

A competitor can build under another name. The domain creates leverage only if the identity is genuinely the obvious or trusted one, and if the owner protects and develops the association.

Do not imply legal exclusivity from domain ownership. Where relevant, mention that trademark, business-name, registrant security, renewal, and handle protection are separate considerations. Keep legal comments general unless the user asks for jurisdiction-specific legal research.

## Cultural Stewardship Guardrails

For culturally specific projects:

- Treat the local identity as a responsibility, not merely a monetizable keyword.
- Keep community legitimacy distinct from technical sophistication.
- Frame payment, AI, or Web3 infrastructure as subordinate rails, not the public meaning of the culture.
- Prefer trust, legibility, accessibility, and consent before payment novelty.
- Avoid suggesting that a project may extract from community culture simply because it controls a name.
- Preserve room for other contributors and future partners while recognizing the owner’s legitimate first-mover position.

A useful test is:

> Does this positioning help the community recognize, participate in, and benefit from the project—or only make the project easier to sell?

## Response Pattern

When the user is expressing an early insight rather than requesting a full strategy document:

1. Acknowledge the actual insight in their terms.
2. Clarify the strategic distinction if needed.
3. State the strongest defensible business thesis in one paragraph.
4. Name the main caveat without flattening the excitement.
5. Offer the few mechanisms that would compound value: secure ownership, credible use, community association, legal hygiene, and relationships.

Keep the response direct and energetic. Light playful tone is welcome when the user sets it, but do not let hype replace analysis.

## Example Application

For a culturally specific domain such as `acadie.sol`, the defensible framing is:

> If sovereign AI, machine payments, and cultural coordination become a meaningful category in Acadia, Acadie.sol already occupies the most natural and culturally specific brand position for that category.

This recognizes domain leverage without claiming that the name alone proves future valuation or grants unilateral cultural authority.

## Verification Checklist

Before finalizing a positioning response, check:

- [ ] Did I distinguish ownership of an identity from authority over a community?
- [ ] Did I explain why the name is strategically specific rather than merely call it valuable?
- [ ] Did I avoid guaranteeing future valuation or profit?
- [ ] Did I identify how value would compound through use and trust?
- [ ] Did I preserve the user’s actual business/branding thesis after any correction?
- [ ] Did I keep the answer proportionate to the user’s request?
